# Academic Style, Argumentation, and Anti-AI Writing Guide

## 1. Governing principle

Write so that a qualified reader can understand:
- What is being claimed.
- Why the claim matters.
- What evidence supports it.
- How the evidence leads to the interpretation.
- Under what conditions the conclusion holds.

Academic quality is not the same as formality, verbosity, ornate vocabulary, or perfectly symmetrical structure.

## 2. Paragraph-level argument

A paragraph should have one dominant communicative job. A useful diagnostic pattern is:

1. **Claim/topic:** the point this paragraph establishes.
2. **Evidence:** data, theory, source, method detail, or example.
3. **Reasoning:** why the evidence supports the point.
4. **Implication/transition:** what follows or what the next paragraph needs to address.

This is a reasoning aid, not a mandatory four-sentence template. Some paragraphs need only a claim and evidence; others need several sentences of explanation.

Check that adjacent paragraphs form a meaningful sequence rather than a collection of related facts.

## 3. Sentence information flow

Gopen and Swan's reader-expectation approach is useful: readers often benefit when a sentence begins with familiar/contextual information and places important new information toward the end. Apply this flexibly.

- Keep the grammatical subject close to the main verb.
- Avoid stacking multiple unrelated claims into one sentence.
- Use a stable topic across adjacent sentences when the argument continues.
- Introduce a new concept before relying on it.
- Put the key new point where it is easy to identify.
- Vary sentence length naturally; do not impose an arbitrary word limit.

## 4. Genre-sensitive rhetorical behavior

### Introduction
Move from the minimum necessary context to a specific research problem. Synthesize relevant literature, identify a defensible gap, state the objective, and preview the contribution. Avoid generic history and broad social-impact claims that do not lead to the problem.

### Literature review
Organize by research question, method family, theory, evidence, or meaningful chronology. Compare studies on common dimensions. Explain disagreements and limitations. Avoid one-study-per-paragraph catalogues.

### Methods
Describe what was actually done and enough detail to understand or reproduce it. Include assumptions, data, parameters, models, metrics, and procedures when relevant. Do not write a development diary.

### Results
Report observed findings accurately. Separate observation from explanation. Preserve negative, null, and mixed results. Do not select only favorable outcomes.

### Discussion
Explain what findings mean, compare with prior work, examine plausible mechanisms, discuss implications and limitations, and avoid claiming more than the evidence supports.

### Conclusion
Answer the research question, state supported contributions, and define boundaries. Do not introduce new results or repeat every section summary.

## 5. Anti-AI patterns and better alternatives

The goal is not to ban individual phrases. Judge whether each phrase performs a real logical function.

| Common weak pattern | Why it weakens writing | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| “With the rapid development of…” | Generic padding | Start with the specific research context or problem |
| Repeated “not X but Y” / “not merely…but also” | Manufactures contrast | State the actual method or distinction directly |
| “First, second, finally” in every paragraph | Mechanical sequencing | Use only when steps are genuinely ordered |
| “It is worth noting that…” | Delays the point | State the finding directly |
| “In summary/To sum up” repeatedly | Repeats content | Give the actual synthesis or remove the sentence |
| “Advanced, efficient, comprehensive, systematic” | Replaces evidence with praise | Specify metric, comparison, scope, and result |
| “Provides a new idea” | Vague contribution | State exactly what method, capability, evidence, or understanding is added |
| “Lays a solid foundation” | Often empty | Identify the concrete reusable result or implication |
| Long explanations of rejected methods | Turns paper into decision diary | Explain only choices that affect validity, reproducibility, fairness, or interpretation |
| Every paragraph has identical length and shape | Artificial rhythm | Let paragraph structure follow the argument |
| Excessive abstract nouns | Hides actors and actions | Use concrete subject–verb–object statements |
| Synonym swapping for technical terms | Breaks conceptual consistency | Keep defined terms stable |
| All results are positive | Distorts research | Report mixed, null, and negative findings honestly |

### 5.1 “Not X but Y” is conditional, not forbidden

Use a contrast only when it:
- Corrects a real misconception.
- Distinguishes concepts that readers may confuse.
- Defines the study's scope.
- Establishes a substantive difference from prior work.

Do not use contrast merely to make a simple statement sound profound.

### 5.2 Do not narrate irrelevant abandoned alternatives

Include a rejected-method discussion when:
- The alternatives are recognized baselines.
- A systematic comparison is part of the research.
- The choice materially affects validity or interpretation.
- Readers need the rationale to reproduce the method.
- The exclusion changes the scope or generality of the conclusion.

Usually omit:
- Unimplemented ideas considered in private.
- Debugging history unrelated to the research claim.
- Untested alternatives described as if a rigorous comparison occurred.
- Explanations included only to show that the author considered many options.

A paper should present the method and the rationale necessary to evaluate it, not every thought that led to the method.

## 6. Evidence-calibrated phrase functions

Use phrases according to their epistemic force.

| Function | Suitable formulations |
|---|---|
| Direct observation | “The results show…”, “The measured value was…” |
| Bounded experimental finding | “Under the tested conditions, the results indicate…” |
| Cautious interpretation | “This pattern may be associated with…” |
| Comparison | “Compared with X under the same evaluation protocol…” |
| Literature synthesis | “Prior studies can be grouped into…”, “These studies differ in…” |
| Limitation | “The current evaluation is limited to…”, “This conclusion has not been tested under…” |
| Theoretical deduction | “Under assumptions A and B, it follows that…” |
| Hypothesis | “We hypothesize that…; this mechanism remains to be tested.” |
| Contribution | “The study develops/evaluates/identifies…” followed by a precise object |
| Future work | “Future work should evaluate…” with a specific unresolved condition |

Avoid using “prove”, “significant”, “optimal”, “general”, “robust”, “safe”, “reliable”, or “first” without the corresponding evidence and scope.

### 6.1 Preserve claim strength and avoid defensive writing

A useful distinction is between **evidence-calibrated caution** and **defensive writing**. Caution is warranted when the evidence, design, assumptions, or tested scope requires it. Defensive writing adds hedges, disclaimers, self-negation, repeated limitations, or hypothetical reviewer objections without such a basis or a material reader need.

Before adding or retaining a qualification, ask:

1. What exact evidence, design feature, assumption, or tested boundary requires this qualification?
2. Does it materially change the reader's interpretation of the claim, its validity, or its reproducibility?
3. Is the information already stated clearly elsewhere?
4. Can the scope be stated directly and positively instead of through a broad negative disclaimer?

If no concrete reason is identified, do not add the qualification. Do not turn a supported finding into a tentative suggestion merely because a reviewer might ask about a broader setting the paper does not claim to cover. Conversely, do not remove a real limitation to make the paper sound more confident.

For material claims, compare the draft with the approved claim-strength baseline in the Pre-writing Dossier. Classify changes as retained, strengthened, narrowed, weakened, withdrawn, or caveat added. Any weakening, narrowing, withdrawal, or new caveat must cite the evidence and explain why it is necessary. If the evidence does not justify the change, preserve the supported claim. If the evidence does justify it and the change affects the central thesis or contribution, obtain user confirmation and update the Dossier/Claim–Evidence Matrix.

Treat reviewer objections as a separate issue list. Screen each for factual validity, relevance, materiality, and evidence. Put only necessary corrections or clarifications into the manuscript; keep response-only matters outside the paper and identify unfounded or inapplicable objections as such. State a genuine limitation once in the most relevant section rather than repeating it as a disclaimer throughout the paper.

Examples:

**Supported finding — preserve its strength**

> Under the tested conditions, method A increased the pass rate from 60% to 85%.

Do not automatically rewrite this as “method A may have improved the pass rate” or append a warning about all untested bridge types if the paper makes no claim about them.

**Real scope boundary — state it precisely**

> The method was evaluated on three bridge configurations within the design ranges specified in this study.

This is a useful scope statement when it reflects the actual evaluation. It is more informative than a generic disclaimer that the method may not work in every possible setting.

If no approved baseline or earlier draft is available, do not claim that the manuscript has been weakened relative to an unseen original. You may still identify redundant or unsupported qualifications by checking the available evidence and the manuscript's own stated scope.

## 7. Chinese academic expression examples

### Direct research statement

Weak:
> 本研究并不是简单地将人工智能技术应用于工程领域，而是构建了一种全面、系统且具有创新性的智能化研究框架。

Better:
> 本研究构建了一个由条件生成模型、参数化结构生成器和有限元评价模块组成的桥梁方案生成流程。

### Focused problem statement

Weak:
> 随着人工智能技术的不断发展，传统桥梁设计方法面临新的机遇和挑战。

Better:
> 现有参数化设计流程通常依赖预设结构形式和人工调整参数，难以直接生成满足多项工程约束的完整结构方案。

### Evidence-based result

Weak:
> 该方法显著提升了模型性能，具有较好的应用前景。

Better:
> 在相同测试条件下，采用该反馈策略后，有限元验算通过率由 X% 提高至 Y%。该结果支持其在当前测试范围内改善结构可行性生成的判断。

Only use the numerical example after verifying the actual values.

### Avoid unsupported generalization

Weak:
> 该方法具有良好的泛化能力，能够适用于各类桥梁结构。

Better:
> 该方法已在本文覆盖的桥型和设计条件下完成验证。其在其他结构体系中的适用性仍需进一步评估。

### Avoid unnecessary decision diary

Weak:
> 研究初期考虑了遗传算法、粒子群算法和多种神经网络，经过多轮分析后最终选择了当前方法。

Better:
> 本研究采用 X 方法建立……关系。该选择主要基于……，并通过……实验评估其有效性。

The better version is appropriate only if the rationale and experiment are real.

## 8. Technical terminology and consistency

- Define key terms once and use them consistently.
- Do not treat related concepts as synonyms unless they are genuinely equivalent in the discipline.
- Keep symbols, subscripts, units, abbreviations, and variable names consistent.
- Define acronyms at first use, following venue conventions.
- Preserve standard disciplinary translations; do not invent elegant but nonstandard translations.
- Distinguish “feasibility”, “safety”, “reliability”, “robustness”, “efficiency”, and “optimality” where the discipline does.

## 9. Figure, table, and equation prose

- Introduce each figure/table in the text and explain the finding it supports.
- Do not restate every cell or every plotted value in prose.
- State the key pattern, comparison, exception, or implication.
- Captions should identify the object and what is shown; notes should define abbreviations and conditions.
- Ensure every axis, unit, legend, color scale, and sample count is interpretable.
- Do not infer a mechanism from a plot unless the data or theory support it.

## 10. English-language academic writing

For English manuscripts:
- Prefer direct subject–verb structure where it improves clarity.
- Use active/passive voice according to disciplinary and journal convention; do not force passive voice.
- Avoid literal translation of Chinese rhetorical patterns.
- Use cautious language where evidence is limited.
- Keep tense consistent with the function: established knowledge, methods performed, results observed, and interpretations.
- Use field-standard terminology and spelling conventions required by the target venue.

## 11. Useful reference resources

Use these as guidance, not as phrase templates to copy mechanically:

- University of Manchester, [Academic Phrasebank](https://www.phrasebank.manchester.ac.uk/): academic communicative functions such as introducing work, reporting results, discussing findings, and expressing caution.
- George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, “The Science of Scientific Writing” (1990): reader expectations, topic position, stress position, and information flow.
- Target journal's current author guidelines: the final authority for venue-specific conventions.

Do not reproduce long copyrighted passages from these resources. Learn the communicative function and write original wording appropriate to the study.
