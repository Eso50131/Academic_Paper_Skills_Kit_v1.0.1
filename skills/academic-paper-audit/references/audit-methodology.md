# Audit Methodology and Decision Rules

## 1. Why two passes?

The first pass examines the manuscript as a standalone scholarly artifact, reducing the chance that the author's explanation or approved outline anchors the reviewer. The second pass tests the manuscript against the Dossier, original materials, and independent sources.

This is a procedural safeguard, not a guarantee of complete independence.

## 2. Pass 1 — blind manuscript review

Before reading the Dossier:
- Read the entire manuscript.
- Identify the apparent genre, question, thesis, method, evidence, and conclusion.
- Record internal inconsistencies and unsupported-looking claims.
- Evaluate narrative flow, section functions, paragraph logic, and visual integration.
- Note claims that require external verification.
- Do not infer missing research facts.
- Note possible repetitive disclaimers or claim weakening only as provisional observations; do not label them defensive before checking the approved baseline and evidence.
- Keep findings provisional until Pass 2.

## 3. Pass 2 — cross-check

Read the Dossier and evidence materials. For each central claim:
1. Locate the manuscript statement.
2. Locate the corresponding Dossier entry.
3. Locate the original evidence.
4. Independently verify external claims where necessary.
5. Compare scope and certainty.
6. Assign evidence status.
7. Assign severity only after considering impact.

If the Dossier and manuscript agree but original evidence conflicts, report the conflict. If original evidence is absent, report the item as unverifiable rather than verified.

### Claim-strength and defensive-writing review

When the Dossier contains an approved core-claim baseline, compare it with the final manuscript. If an earlier draft is supplied, compare that version as well. For each material change, identify whether the claim was retained, strengthened, narrowed, weakened, withdrawn, or given a new caveat. Verify whether the change follows from actual evidence, a real study boundary, or a material reader need.

Do not treat caution itself as a defect. A limitation is appropriate when it is real and relevant; a hedge is appropriate when the evidence warrants uncertainty. A finding is warranted only when a qualification or weakening lacks such a basis, is repeated without adding information, or materially obscures an evidence-supported result. If no baseline is available, do not infer that a prior claim was weakened; assess only what can be established from the final manuscript and supplied evidence.

## 4. Severity and impact

### Critical
Examples:
- Central result demonstrably contradicts original data.
- A central experiment/method is represented as completed but records show it was not performed.
- A fabricated or materially false citation supports a central claim.
- A core conclusion is invalidated by decisive evidence.
- Serious integrity concern supported by concrete evidence.

### Major
Examples:
- A core method is materially misdescribed.
- A central result or novelty claim lacks necessary evidence.
- A key comparison is invalid or unfair.
- The main conclusion substantially exceeds the experiments.
- A major part of the approved scope is omitted or changed without justification.

### Moderate
Examples:
- Important literature synthesis is incomplete.
- A mechanism is asserted without adequate support but is not the sole basis of the main conclusion.
- A key assumption or limitation is insufficiently discussed.
- A section's logic or reproducibility detail is materially weak.

### Minor
Examples:
- Local terminology inconsistency.
- Caption, numbering, formatting, grammar, or clarity issue.
- Repetitive or mechanical phrasing that does not change the argument.

Do not escalate a missing source to Critical merely because it is missing. Consider centrality, available contrary evidence, and whether the claim can be checked.

## 5. Verdict decision tree

1. Is the audit package adequate to judge the central claims?
   - If no, use `Unable to Complete` and list the missing evidence.
2. Is there an unresolved Critical finding?
   - If yes, use `Fail`.
3. Is there an unresolved Major finding?
   - If yes, use `Fail`.
4. Are central claims supported within their stated scope and no Critical/Major findings remain?
   - If yes, consider `Pass` or `Conditional Pass`.
5. Use `Conditional Pass` when only bounded, non-critical conditions remain and the manuscript's central integrity is sound.
6. Use `Pass` only when the required checks are adequately completed within the declared scope and no blocking findings remain.

Do not average categories into one score. A strong language score cannot compensate for invalid data.

## 6. Plan compliance versus independent scholarly judgment

Evaluate these separately:

- **Plan compliance:** Did the manuscript follow the approved Research Brief and outline? Were approved claims, scope, and exclusions respected?
- **Scholarly validity:** Is the plan itself defensible? Does the literature support the gap? Does the method answer the question? Does the evidence support the conclusion?

A manuscript can follow its approved outline and still be scientifically weak. Conversely, a justified change from the outline may be acceptable if documented and supported.

## 7. Audit boundaries

The report must state:
- Which files and sources were actually inspected.
- Whether code was executed or only read.
- Whether raw data were recalculated or only summarized.
- Whether full text or abstract-only sources were reviewed.
- Whether images were visually inspected.
- Whether the target journal's current instructions were checked.
- Any unavailable tools, inaccessible sources, or unresolved uncertainty.

Do not claim plagiarism detection, data-forensics certification, peer-review prediction, or AI-detector verification unless a suitable method/tool was actually used and its limitations are reported.

## 8. Re-audit protocol

When a revised manuscript arrives:
- Treat it as a new version.
- Compare it with the prior audited version.
- Re-check every Critical/Major finding.
- Check dependent claims, tables, figures, and conclusions.
- Look for regressions and newly introduced unsupported claims.
- Update the finding log; do not erase unresolved history.
- State whether the previous verdict is superseded.
